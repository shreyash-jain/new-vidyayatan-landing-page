Almost every part of school accreditation now happens on a screen. The school applies online. The evaluators score it online. The certificate arrives as a file, and when a parent scans the seal on their phone, something behind the scenes has to confirm it is real. The one part that is still entirely human is the judgement itself — and even that reaches the panel through software somebody had to build.

## What the Conclave is

Presented by [IIRF](https://iirfranking.com/), the International Institutional Ranking Framework, the ICOSA School Conclave 2026 follows the first ICOSA Conclave and Awards, held at the same venue on 11 December 2025. This year's theme is *Beyond Accreditation, Towards Transformation*. School leaders, policymakers and academics spend a day on one question: what is accreditation actually for?

It is worth asking, because the usual answer is a poor one. For most schools, accreditation ends the day the certificate arrives. ICOSA's position is that it shouldn't. Its evaluators are working educators, and they are meant to hand back a plan for getting better rather than a list of faults. Its benchmarks are international, but they are meant to sit alongside a school's own character instead of flattening it.

## What ICOSA measures

ICOSA publishes its framework in full, which is rarer than it should be. Every school is assessed twice over: once by an ICOSA expert panel, and once by the school itself. Both use a 5-point scale, and **the final grade is the average of the two**. Six areas carry 100 marks each — academic performance, teacher and teaching competence, teaching resources and pedagogy, sports and infrastructure, 21st-century skills, and innovative curriculum. Grades run from Foundational (2.0–2.50) up to Excellence (3.51–5.0). It happens entirely online, takes 7 to 10 days, and ends with a digital certificate a parent can check by scanning a QR code, plus a listing in ICOSA's public directory. Schools in more than 15 countries have been through it.

One detail is easy to skim past: the school supplies half of its own grade. That is deliberate, not a loophole. But it means the most revealing number is not the final grade at all — it is the **gap between what the school scored itself and what the panel scored it**. A school that rates itself well above its panel isn't necessarily being dishonest, but that is the file a reviewer opens first. So a system built to run this framework has to keep both scores side by side for every single question, rather than averaging them together and losing the difference. ## The AI-readiness assessment is the part worth watching

ICOSA has also launched a second, separate track: **AI-Enabled School Readiness Accreditation**, which it calls a first of its kind. Forty-eight questions across six areas — infrastructure, curriculum, faculty, student outcomes, governance and innovation — produce a score ICOSA calls the AI Index, marked out of 100. Schools have 30 days to complete it, and are recognised as an AI-Enabled School at 2.5 out of 5 or above.

![ICOSA's AI-Enabled School Readiness Accreditation: forty-eight assessment questions across six dimensions — infrastructure, curriculum, faculty, student outcomes, governance and innovation — producing a 0 to 100 ICOSA AI Index, with recognition at 2.5 out of 5 or above](/images/blog/icosa-ai-readiness.svg)

When most schools say they are "AI-ready", they mean they bought a chatbot licence and held a staff briefing. Scoring governance and student outcomes as well as equipment is much harder to talk your way through, and it forces the uncomfortable questions into the open. Who signs off on using AI to mark a child's work? [What happens to that work once it sits inside a supplier's system](/blog/dpdp-act-schools-edtech-compliance)? Can anyone actually show the tool helped? Those are the questions we spend our weeks on, which is roughly why this partnership exists.

## What software can't fix

A certificate is only worth what people believe about it, and belief is not something you can build. "Entirely online, in 7 to 10 days" is a real advantage for a principal with no spare week — but to a doubtful parent it can simply sound *easy*. And a process that is quick to award an accreditation but never takes one back becomes a problem the first time an accredited school is in the news for the wrong reasons.

More dashboards will not fix that. Three things will. Every score a panel gives should be traceable back to the specific evidence behind it. The standards should stay fixed once a certificate has been issued, so this year's grade still means the same thing next year. And it has to be possible to withdraw an accreditation and have the QR code say so the moment a parent scans it.

Software has a narrow job in all this: make the judgement easy to see and easy to check. It does not make the judgement.
