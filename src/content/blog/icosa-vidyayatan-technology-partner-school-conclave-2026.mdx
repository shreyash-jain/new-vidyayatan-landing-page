## What the Conclave is

Presented by [IIRF](https://iirfranking.com/), the International Institutional Ranking Framework, the ICOSA School Conclave 2026 follows the inaugural ICOSA Conclave and Awards held at the same venue on 11 December 2025. This year's theme is *Beyond Accreditation, Towards Transformation* — school leaders, policymakers and academics in a room for a day, arguing about what accreditation is actually for in the 10+2 ecosystem.

That argument is worth having, because the default answer is a bad one. Most accreditation ends at the certificate. ICOSA's stated position is that it shouldn't: evaluators are practising educators who hand back a growth roadmap, not an audit report, and international benchmarks are meant to sit alongside a school's own heritage rather than flatten it.

## What ICOSA measures

The framework is published openly, which is rarer than it should be. Two parallel evaluations — an ICOSA expert panel and a school self-assessment — each scored on a 5-point scale, **with the final grade being the average of the two**. Six pillars carry 100 marks each: academic performance, teacher and teaching competence, teaching resources and pedagogy, sports and infrastructure, 21st-century skills, and innovative curriculum. Bands run from Foundational (2.0–2.50) to Excellence (3.51–5.0). The whole process is online, in 7–10 days, ending in a digital certificate with QR verification and a place in ICOSA's directory. Schools across 15+ countries have gone through it.

Read that as an engineer and one line stands out: the applicant supplies half of its own grade. Not a flaw — a design decision. It makes the **gap between the self-score and the panel score** the most informative number in the file, and any platform running this framework has to store that divergence per criterion rather than averaging it away. We wrote up how that data model works in our [guide to building an accreditation platform](/blog/school-accreditation-software-guide).

## The AI-readiness assessment is the part worth watching

ICOSA has launched a second, separate track: **AI-Enabled School Readiness Accreditation**, which it describes as a first-of-its-kind multi-dimensional AI readiness evaluation. Forty-eight assessment questions across six dimensions — infrastructure, curriculum, faculty, student outcomes, governance, innovation — producing a 0–100 ICOSA AI Index inside a 30-day window, with recognition as an AI-Enabled School at a score of 2.5 out of 5 or above.

![ICOSA's AI-Enabled School Readiness Accreditation: forty-eight assessment questions across six dimensions — infrastructure, curriculum, faculty, student outcomes, governance and innovation — producing a 0 to 100 ICOSA AI Index, with recognition at 2.5 out of 5 or above](/images/blog/icosa-ai-readiness.svg)

Most schools that say they are "AI-ready" mean they bought a chatbot licence and ran a staff briefing. Scoring governance and student outcomes alongside infrastructure is harder to game, and it puts the awkward questions on the record: who approves a model's use in assessment, [what happens to a student's work once it goes into a vendor's system](/blog/dpdp-act-schools-edtech-compliance), and whether anyone can tell if the tool improved anything. Those are the questions we spend our week on, which is roughly why the partnership exists.

## What software can't fix

A credential is worth what people believe about it, and belief is not an engineering deliverable. "Fully online, 7–10 days" is a genuine advantage for a principal with no spare week — but to a sceptical parent it can read as *easy*, and a fast process that never revokes anything is a liability the first time an accredited school has an incident. The fix is not more dashboard. It is a panel whose scores can be traced back to specific evidence, a rubric version that stays fixed once a certificate is issued, and revocation that actually works when someone scans the code.

Technology's job in accreditation is narrow: make the judgement legible and checkable. It does not make the judgement.
